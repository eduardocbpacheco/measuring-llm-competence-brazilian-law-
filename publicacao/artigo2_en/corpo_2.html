3Materials and methods

3.1The two tasks and what each one demands

The evaluation set consists of 105 questions from the second stage of the OAB Bar Exam, drawn from editions 39, 40 and 41, 35 from each. They cover seven areas of law with exactly 15 questions each, so that no area dominates the mean. There are 84 discursive questions and 21 practical cases that ask for a legal document to be drafted, and the two genres demand different things.

The discursive question presents a short case, a median of 182 words, and asks two to four objective questions of law about it. An administrative law case from edition 41 describes the expropriation of rural property by the federal government and asks whether the public authority must make an offer of compensation before filing suit and whether arbitration is available in the procedure. The expected answer is short and the guidelines break it into few criteria, a median of 4 and at most 7: one requires the correct thesis, "the public authority must notify the owner and present an offer of compensation before filing", worth 0.5 points, and another requires the legal basis, "under Art. 10-A of Decree-Law 3,365/1941", worth 0.1. What is demanded is legal content, with no documentary form to satisfy.

Drafting a legal document presents a longer case, a median of 399 words, and asks for a complete procedural document written as counsel for one of the parties. The guidelines break the document into many more criteria, a median of 26 and up to 38, and these come labelled with a title. In a writ of mandamus against an act of the chair of a procurement committee, the first criteria are "Addressing: the document must be addressed to the Civil Court or the Public Treasury Court of the district", "Identification of the parties: respondent authority, João da Silva, chair of the Procurement Committee" and "Admissibility: there was a violation of a clear and certain right". Beyond legal content, the skeleton of the document is demanded.

3.2The two classes of criterion in document drafting

The titles with which the board labels the document criteria fall into two families. Those of form cover the skeleton of the document: addressing to the competent court, identification of the parties, timeliness, admissibility requirements, closing with place, date and signature. Those of substance cover the legal thesis, the grounds and the requests. There are 148 and 413 of them respectively, and every formal criterion is in a document, because discursive questions have no documentary skeleton to satisfy. Grouping the titles into the two families is ours; the titles are not. The split matters because the two classes demand different cognitive operations, both from whoever writes and from whoever grades. Deciding whether the document was addressed to the competent court is matching a stretch of text against a short key. Deciding whether there was a violation of a clear and certain right is assessing a thesis. There is no reason to assume a single model is equally good at both, and Section 4.3 shows it is not.

3.3The three roles and the measurement protocol

Each of the 31 models was measured in the three roles. As a respondent, it receives the case and produces the answer or the document, at temperature 0.7, up to 2,500 output tokens and three runs per question. The answers are graded by the two judges selected by this protocol, Kimi K2.5 on discursive questions and GPT-OSS 120B on documents, with the same judging procedure described below, and the score is the percentage of the board's points, weighted by the points of each criterion. These two judges are also respondents and grade their own answers, a self-preference risk [1] that we report and do not correct. As a judge, it receives another model's answer, the board's answer guidelines and the full list of criteria for that question, without the case statement, and returns in a single structured JSON response a binary verdict for each criterion. The verdicts are compared against the gold standard of 940 decisions taken by 3 experienced lawyers on answers written by GPT-4o mini, both inherited from Rabula [5]. Appendix A gives the judging prompt, Appendix B a complete item with the human and automatic verdicts, Appendix C the configuration of every judge and Appendix D the grouping of criterion titles into classes. Agreement is measured by Cohen's κ, separately by genre, by class of criterion and by area of law.

The whole protocol was repeated up to 5 times per candidate. Judges run at temperature 0.2 and without a seed, which the Amazon Bedrock Converse API, serving 29 of the 31 judges, does not accept, and each repetition is a majority vote over three samples; the five Claude models, re-run under a lower quota, have a single sample, and where only two samples are valid a tie counts as met. The output ceiling depends on the model: 2,048 tokens for Llama 3 and the smaller Mistral models, 16,000 for reasoning models and 8,000 for the rest, doubled when an answer is cut. The prompt template did not change between repetitions, but every call carries a random token and asks for the sum of two random integers, a cache-buster inherited from Rabula that prevents the provider from returning a stored answer. The judging code received retry and output-ceiling fixes during the runs, none touching the prompt. The repetitions therefore measure the dispersion of κ under sampling, which is the variation a user of the same judge faces, and from it the decision threshold used below is derived. Coverage is not uniform across candidates: 26 judges completed the 5 repetitions, three completed three because of API quota, and two produced no usable result on document drafting. A judge is scored only if it covers at least 85% of the criteria of the genre, and only if what is missing is missing by chance, which is checked by Fisher's exact test on the table of judged against omitted by positive against negative human answer.
