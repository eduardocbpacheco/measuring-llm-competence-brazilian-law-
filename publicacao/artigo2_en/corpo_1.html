Measuring the competence of LLMs in Brazilian law

Eduardo Pacheco · Cibele Maria Russo

Institute of Mathematics and Computer Sciences, University of São Paulo
Contact: eduardo.caruso.pacheco@usp.br · Working version, 14 September 2026

Abstract

Evaluating open-ended legal answers at scale depends on using a language model as the grader, and choosing which model will grade is done by measuring its agreement with human annotation and ordering the candidates in a table. We measure 31 models in the three roles this arrangement involves, over the same 105 questions from the second stage of the OAB Bar Exam and the same 940 criteria of the official grading guidelines, with a gold standard from 3 legal experts: as respondents on the discursive question, as respondents on drafting a legal document, and as judges of the other models' answers. The three orderings are reported by area of law.

There are 3 results. Judging and answering are associated competences, but the association weakens as the task stops demanding form and starts demanding substance: ρ = 0.67 on the discursive question, 0.61 on the formal criteria of the document and 0.41 on the substantive ones. Drafting a document is not a single task: split into the two classes of criterion the examining board itself labels, the judge's agreement is higher on the formal part than on the substantive one for 25 of the 26 judges measured, with a median difference of 0.168, the two orderings agree only at ρ = 0.48, and the leader is not the same. And a single number per judge hides more than it shows: among the top nine, whom the protocol's noise floor ties within a span of 0.047, a single judge's agreement varies across areas of law with a median of 0.279, almost 6 times that span.

Keywords: Benchmark · LLM-as-a-Judge · Brazil · Law · Reproducibility

1Introduction

Evaluating whether a language model answered an open legal question well is expensive. There is no single answer key, and string comparison does not work, because two correct answers may not share a word. The human alternative, hiring lawyers to read every answer, does not scale to dozens of models over hundreds of questions. The route the field has taken is to use a language model as the grader, the technique known as LLM-as-judge [1]. The grader is given the question, the answer under evaluation and the grading criterion, and is asked for a verdict. This makes scale possible, but pushes the problem one step back: how do we know the grader grades well? The standard answer is to calibrate it against human annotation. Lawyers annotate a sample, the agreement between each candidate model and that annotation is measured, and the one that agrees most is chosen. Agreement is measured by Cohen's κ [7], the proportion of matches discounted by the agreement expected by chance. The discount matters: if 80% of criteria have the answer "not met", a judge that always answers "not met" matches 80% while knowing nothing, and its κ is zero. By the field's convention, κ above 0.80 counts as strong agreement. This arrangement puts models in three distinct roles over the same material: drafting a legal document, answering a discursive question, and judging what the others wrote. Current practice treats the three as a single competence, and that is what licenses choosing the judge by its performance on the task, without human annotation.

This work measures the same 31 models in the three roles, over the same set of questions and the same grading guidelines, and reports each role by area of law. The three results that follow are consequences of that joint measurement, and none of them appears in an aggregate table of one number per model. The verdicts of the 31 candidates over the 940 criteria, in up to 5 repetitions each, and the annotations of the three legal experts that form the gold standard are deposited at 10.5281/zenodo.22739161 [11], with a checksum per file. The training corpus and the judgments of the 180 conditions of the companion study [10] are at 10.5281/zenodo.22739216 [12] and 10.5281/zenodo.22739184 [13], and the code at [14].

2Related work

The use of models as evaluators was systematised by Zheng et al. [1], who also documented biases of the procedure, of position, of verbosity and of self-preference. Later work measured the judge's agreement with humans in specific domains and proposed corrections, but the reporting format stayed the same: an agreement table, ordered, with one number per judge. In legal evaluation, LegalBench [2] avoids the problem by using short, verifiable answer tasks, at the cost of moving away from professional practice.

For legal Portuguese there are 3 directly relevant evaluation sets, all built on real examinations, and two of them on the same one we use. oab-bench [3] draws 105 questions across seven areas of law from recent editions of the OAB Bar Exam and grades them on a 0–10 scale against the examiners' guidelines, reporting a strong correlation between a frontier model's scores and human ones. Magis-Bench [4] moves to the judiciary, with 74 questions from eight competitive examinations for judicial positions between 2023 and 2025, covering discursive analysis and the drafting of civil and criminal judgments, graded by four frontier models acting as judges. Rabula [5] is built on the 2024 OAB Bar Exam with four tasks, grades the generative ones against the board's own criteria as binary questions weighted by difficulty, and calibrates the automatic judge by Cohen's agreement with 3 legal experts. From the last of these we inherit the evaluation set, the human gold standard and the grading procedure, without modifying them.

All three report model competence as an aggregate score per task, and Rabula also reports the judge's agreement as one number per candidate. None of them splits document drafting into the classes of criterion the board labels, nor reports agreement by area of law, nor measures the same models as respondents and as judges to compare the two orderings. On the reproducibility of inference, it is known that the non-associativity of floating-point arithmetic makes the result depend on how operations are grouped [6]; what we did not find in the LLM-as-judge literature is the recognition that this makes batch size a parameter of the protocol, alongside temperature and seed.
